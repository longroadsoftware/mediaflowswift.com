MediaFlowSwift vs Just Using Finder and a Spreadsheet

Finder and a spreadsheet cost nothing and need no new app. What they don't do is prove a copy is safe. A drag that finishes without an error can still leave a damaged file. Nothing tells you until you try to use it. MediaFlowSwift's checksum checks exist to catch that case.

Why isn't a successful copy in Finder proof enough?

Dragging files in Finder moves the bytes and tells you when it's done. It does not compute a checksum of the original and compare it with one of the copy. So it can't tell a perfect copy from one with a few flipped bits. A damaged file can still have the right name and size. It can even open and play for a few seconds before it stutters or goes black.

The hash functions that catch this are not something Finder's Copy command runs. One of them is SHA-256, set out in the US government's Secure Hash Standard.

How do you check a copy by hand?

You can do the manual way properly. It just takes steps that are easy to skip on a busy day:

  1. Compare the item count and total size of the card folder and the copy in Finder's Get Info. This catches a missing file, but not a damaged one.
  2. Run shasum -a 256 in Terminal on each file on the card, then on each copy. Compare the two lists line by line. This catches a damaged file.
  3. Write the card, the drive and the date in your spreadsheet.
  4. Keep the card unformatted until a second copy exists somewhere else.

Step 2 is the one that proves anything. It also means reading the card and the copy in full, which is the slow part.

How long does a proper check take?

Take a full 128 GB card and a setup that moves data at 250 MB/s. One full read of the card is 128,000 MB ÷ 250 MB/s = 512 seconds, about 8.5 minutes. Copying it, then reading both sides to compare, is three passes, roughly 25 minutes. That is arithmetic, not a benchmark. The real figure depends on the slowest of the card, the reader and the drive.

The time is the same whether you run the check or an app does. The difference is who has to remember to run it. MediaFlowSwift reads the copy back on every import without being asked. It takes the card's checksum while it copies, so its import is two passes, about 17 minutes.

What does a spreadsheet miss that an app like this keeps track of?

A spreadsheet can record which card went where. But it only knows what you remember to type into it. It does not look at your drives to check that what it says is still true. It won't notice a renamed drive, a moved folder, or a copy that stopped partway through.

MediaFlowSwift keeps that record from the files themselves. Each clip shows whether it is on the card, in a working copy, at the project's destination, or archived. A copy that fails its check is never marked as if it succeeded.

It does not follow a move on its own, though. A file moved or renamed outside the app shows as Missing until you relink it. So the change is flagged instead of slipping by.

When is the manual way good enough?

Finder is fine for a handful of clips you will edit the same day. The same goes for a card you are in no hurry to reuse. The risk of a bad copy going unnoticed is low, and checking by eye costs little. The manual way gets riskier as the stakes rise:

  • more cards and more projects than you can remember;
  • footage you cannot reshoot, such as an event or a trip;
  • a card you need to format and hand back today;
  • archive drives on a shelf that someone else may need to search.

That is the point where an unchecked copy becomes an expensive mistake, not an inconvenience.
