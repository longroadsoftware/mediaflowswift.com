Who makes MediaFlowSwift, and why

MediaFlowSwift is made by Long Road Software LLC, a small company in Tennessee that publishes the app and supports it directly. The person behind it is Mike, a video creator who wrote the app for his own footage and runs his own channel on it.

Where it came from

Mike also makes On Island Time, a YouTube channel about living and travelling in an RV. The cameras added up. Two GoPros, then a third, and on top of that two iPhones and a Nikon.

For a long time the footage went up to the GoPro service and came back down. Grouping projects was a mess. Uploading a large number of files could take days. Errors were common, and so was lost footage. The only confirmation that an upload had finished was on the camera itself, and the errors along the way left real doubt about whether every clip had made it.

The iPhones were a separate mess. Getting video and pictures off them was hard, and he never quite found a way to do it that he trusted.

He bought an expensive NAS next. That made offloading a bit less hard. It did not fix the rest. He still had to organize the videos into projects. Looking through them took forever, and it could lock him into that work for hours, sometimes days, before there was one project he could import and edit.

MediaFlowSwift came out of that frustration. It records where every clip is — on a card, in a working copy, at the project’s destination, or on a numbered archive drive — copies footage into folders ready for editing, and clears space only once the copy has been read back and proved to match. It is sold to other creators because the mess is not special to one channel.

What it promises about your files

The promise is narrow, and it is kept literally. Whenever MediaFlowSwift copies footage and then removes the original — clearing a card, freeing up space after Organize, moving a project to an editing drive and back — the copy is checked against the original first, and if the two do not match, nothing is removed. Your footage stays in ordinary folders that any app can read, on the drives and network shares you chose. The app has no account and collects no usage data; apart from checking for updates and checking your licence key, it talks to no outside service until you turn one on, and Settings › Privacy lists what each one sends. When something fails, it says which file, why, and what to do. That is the whole promise: not that drives never fail, but that nothing is removed on a guess.
